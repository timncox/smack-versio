Catch what you just played. Cut it up. Let a seed decide the rest.

⚠ Early firmware — played, but not finished

It runs on a module. Audio, capture, the knob layout and the effects are all confirmed by ear, it has been played hard without audible trouble, and the clock adapter, settings layer, allocator and DSP engine have passing native test suites. What has not been read yet is the boot CPU report — the actual number the design rests on, as opposed to the absence of complaints from one session.

Expect rough edges, and expect to flash again. Throughout this manual, cyan marks what has been measured or verified and amber marks what is assumed. Read the colours.

What it does

01 — The idea

Audio runs through the module continuously and is always being recorded. Press CAPTURE and the last N steps of what you just played become a loop — no arming, no deciding in advance that you wanted it.

Retro-capture

You never miss it

The good take is usually the one you weren't recording. The buffer is always rolling, so CAPTURE reaches backwards instead of forwards.

Seeded

One knob, whole arrangement

Which slices get re-ordered, which get an effect, and which effect — all follow from a single SEED. Turn it for a different arrangement of the same audio. Same seed, same result, every time.

Density, not editing

Steer the statistics

The Move version has 23 pads and a screen for per-slice editing. Seven knobs cannot do that, so this steers the probabilities instead — how much re-ordering, how many effects.

It is a port of Smack, a module for the Ableton Move. The DSP engine is the same code, vendored unmodified — so the sound is not a reimplementation of the original, it is the original.

Playing it

03 — Five steps to the first loop
1  Patch audio to IN L / IN R. It passes through immediately —
   the module is not silent before you touch it.
2  Patch a clock to CLK. No clock? It free-runs. That is fine.
3  Play something.
4  Hold CAPTURE about a second. The last few bars become a loop
   and start playing.
5  Bring up FX and ORDER. Turn SEED until you like it.

Tap CAPTURE to re-roll — same loop, new pattern. Re-roll is on the tap because it is the gesture you use constantly while playing; grabbing a loop is deliberate and happens once, so it gets the hold. Hold past two seconds to drop the loop and return to dry passthrough.

LIVE mode — double-tap CAPTURE

STATE turns cyan. Instead of repeating one frozen take, the module re-captures itself once per loop pass, so the buffer keeps refilling with what you are playing now.

LENGTH sets how often that happens, and the wait is a full loop pass. At 120 BPM that is about 1 s at 8 steps, 4 s at 32, and 32 s at 256 — where it looks completely dead for half a minute. If you want LIVE to feel live, turn LENGTH down. That is the control.

It records your dry input, never the effected output, so passes never compound and the loop cannot degrade into mush. What it cannot be is a true insert: reverse, tapestop, scratch, retrig and freeze all work on audio that has already happened, so one loop pass is the floor, not a shortcut.

Very slow clocks shorten the longest LENGTH. A loop may take at most half the module's 150-second buffer, because the recorder has to keep writing a fresh loop alongside the one that is playing. Below about 51 BPM — which CLK at ×2 reaches from a 100 BPM clock — LENGTH drops a notch rather than being trimmed off-grid. It stays dropped until the knob moves, so sweep LENGTH and back if it seems stuck short.

LENGTH, SLICE and SEED are stepped, and a stepped control parked exactly on a boundary would otherwise chatter between two values as CV noise nudges it back and forth. Each has a deadband to stop that. SEED's is deliberately narrower than the others — a single step of SEED re-rolls the whole pattern, so it needs the protection, but a wide band would make the knob skip most of the 128 seeds.

The clock

04 — One jack, two jobs

The Versio has exactly one gate jack, and this module wants two things from it: a tempo, and a "capture now" trigger. The GATE switch decides which.

Switch right

CLK

The jack is a clock. Tempo comes from the pulse intervals.

Switch centre

Auto

Worked out from what arrives — a steady train reads as a clock, sporadic hits read as triggers. feel unverified

Switch left

Trig

The jack is a capture trigger. Tempo is inferred from the gaps between hits.

Internally the module turns gate edges into 24 ppqn MIDI clock, because that is what the engine already speaks — so the timing model that runs on the Move runs here untouched. This is the piece with the most design risk in the whole port, and it is also the most thoroughly tested: verified a synthetic gate train drives the real engine to capture a bar of 95,985 frames against a 96,000 target, an error of 0.02%.

With nothing patched it free-runs — at the last tempo it successfully locked to, remembered across power cycles, rather than a fixed 120.

Reading the LEDs

05 — Four lights are the entire display

All four solid red means the engine could not allocate its buffer. That should be impossible — it needs 27.63 MB of a 32 MB pool, measured — but it fails loudly rather than running silently broken.

⚠ Do not flash v0.1.0

v0.1.0 hard-faults on boot. It was tagged before the firmware had ever run on hardware, and the first module to run it produced only a steady buzz — the audio callback overran its buffer on every block, so it emitted noise rather than anything you patched into it. The download above is left in place only so the checksums on the release stay honest.

Wait for v0.2.0, which is what this manual describes — or build from source in the meantime.

⚠ Before you flash it

This is early firmware. It has been played on a module — audio, capture, the effects — but it is not finished, and the CPU budget under heavy settings is still unconfirmed. Expect to flash again.

It is third-party firmware. Noise Engineering did not write it and cannot support it, so please do not contact them about it. Installing is fully reversible: flashing any official NE firmware returns the module to stock.

If you run it, the boot CPU reading is the one thing worth reporting back.

Installing

06 — And getting back to stock

The micro-USB port is on the back of the Daisy Seed, so the module comes out of the case. Expect that in the edit loop.

# once — the Daisy bootloader (hold BOOT, tap RESET, release BOOT)
make -C firmware program-boot

# every time — power-cycle first, then while the bootloader waits
make -C firmware program-dfu

This is a BOOT_SRAM build. The app is ~142 KB and the STM32H750 has only 128 KB of internal flash, so it runs from SRAM via the bootloader. Only 26 KB of that is this project — the rest is libDaisy and the ST HAL. It is also why the two reference firmwares are 85–91 KB: they were built to fit internal flash.

Everything here is reversible. Flashing any official Noise Engineering firmware overwrites internal flash and returns the module to stock. Nothing is cut, soldered, or one-way.

The faceplate

07 — Where these coordinates came from

Your module's panel says whatever it said before, so the repository generates a printable overlay with the right words on it.

python3 faceplate/make_faceplate.py

The geometry was derived, not measured. Page 5 of the FRGMNTS Versio manual is a 1:1 print template; converting it to vectors gives exact hole centres, because a bezier circle's control points share its bounding box.

self-validating The reason to trust that at all is what fell out of it: the derived panel outline is 50.44 × 128.34 mm — 10 HP by 3 U — and the four mounting slots land 2.99 mm and 125.33 mm from the top edge, 7.60 mm from the left. That is the Eurorack mechanical standard to a hundredth of a millimetre, from a transform that was never fitted to it. It also independently confirms the control surface: twelve jacks in three rows of four is 7 CV + gate + 4 audio.

corrected by hand One hole never came out of the extraction at all. The only button-sized candidate in the artwork was decoration, and both attempts to derive the button from its neighbours failed the overlap check — one placing it 0.6 mm inside a switch, the other 0.07 mm into the top jack row.

Those failures turned out to be the signal rather than the noise. The left column kept refusing to fit a button because there is no button in the left column — it sits in the centre, level with the lower switch, which is what the module actually shows. A constraint that will not close is sometimes telling you the premise is wrong rather than the arithmetic. It is now placed from the hardware, by eye; MEASURE.md still asks for calipers before anyone machines a panel.

Print the print sheet at 100% and check the 50 mm ruler on it with a real ruler before cutting. That ruler exists because every way printing goes wrong — "fit to page", silently scaling to Letter — is invisible except as a ruler that is not 50 mm.
